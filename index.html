How it works

In 1725 Johann Joseph Fux published Gradus ad Parnassum ("steps to Parnassus"), a dialogue in which a master teaches a pupil to write counterpoint in five graded species. It taught Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven, and it is still taught today. Gradus composes the first four species.

  1. A cantus firmus is written first: a plain melody in whole notes that starts and ends on the final of the mode, moves mostly by step, has one high point and recovers from its leaps.
  2. The counterpoint is found by a beam search. The composer writes left to right. At each step it extends the best few dozen partial lines by every pitch the rules allow, scores them on taste (contrary motion, thirds and sixths, few leaps, and suspensions in the fourth species), and keeps the best. If that greedy search walks into a dead end, an exhaustive search with memory takes over. It finds a solution or proves that none exists.
  3. The rules live in one module, and the composer and the critic share it. So when you change a note, you get exactly the judgement the composer applied to itself. Why this note? tries every alternative pitch in place and reports what each would break.
  4. The singing is synthesised in the page: harmonics shaped by vowel formants ("ah" above, "oh" below), vibrato that blooms after each attack, and a stone-room reverb. It is tuned in quarter-comma meantone, the tuning of a sixteenth-century organ, where major thirds are pure.

The master's remarks paraphrase the traditional rules. They are not quotations from Fux.
